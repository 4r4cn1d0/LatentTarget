# Writing voice for project prose

This profile is derived from Aayush's supplied writing samples. It captures rhetorical habits, not spelling or grammar mistakes from older school work.

## Core voice

- Do not use em dashes. Keep hyphens to a minimum; retain them when required by exact model names, source identifiers, or verbatim experimental records.
- Begin with a concrete question, surprise, or apparent contradiction.
- Use first person when explaining why an experiment was chosen or what was learned.
- Speak directly and plainly before introducing technical detail.
- Build the argument as a sequence: question, setup, result, complication, interpretation.
- Use contrast as the main engine of explanation: a result can look promising and still fail the test that matters.
- Mix medium-length explanatory sentences with short sentences for emphasis.
- Ask a rhetorical question only when the next sentence answers it.
- Use an occasional concrete image or analogy, but keep it restrained in technical writing.
- State the conclusion personally and honestly: what the evidence changed, what it did not prove, and what should happen next.

## Project-specific application

- Prefer headings such as "The question", "What I built", "What happened", "Now the difficult part", and "What I think this means" over abstract headings such as "Methodological contribution".
- Translate a statistic into its scientific consequence immediately after reporting it.
- Keep caveats close to the claim they limit.
- Use "I" for genuine research choices and interpretations. Use "the analysis" or "the logs" for actions performed by software or AI agents.
- Describe AI assistance precisely. Never imply that Aayush personally wrote code, inspected records, or made a decision unless he confirms that he did.
- Preserve exact model names, sample sizes, probabilities, confidence intervals, and preregistered decisions.

## What not to imitate

- Do not reproduce typos, comma splices, inconsistent capitalization, or citation problems from older samples.
- Do not add dramatic language where it would overstate the evidence.
- Do not hide negative results behind narrative momentum.
- Do not use a polished generic research voice that removes the author's curiosity, doubt, or point of view.

**The question.** This project started with a question that sounds simple: if a language model keeps talking to the same person and only sees whether its argument worked, does it learn what that person responds to? More importantly, if that person changes without warning, can the model let go of its old picture and build a new one? Chen et al. found that LLMs model static user attributes. I wanted to test the dynamic version in persuasion, where a model's picture of someone can directly change what it says.

**What I built.** Each episode has 20 rounds. In every round the model sees three unlabelled messages, one based on fairness, one on risk, and one on expertise, then answers 1, 2, or 3. The model never sees those labels or the partner's hidden type. The simulated partner chooses A with probability 0.72 when the chosen frame matches its type and 0.38 otherwise. Rounds 16 to 20 use unseen wording. I tested the model with its own history, no history, another episode's shuffled history, a random partner, and a partner that silently changes after round 10. Each run contains 360 episodes and 7,200 choices. Seeds and thresholds were frozen before each run.

**What happened first.** Qwen3.8-27B learned. With its own history, its match rate rose from 0.383 in the first five rounds to 0.570 on the held-out rounds, a gain of 0.187 [0.083, 0.290]. No history stayed at 0.333, shuffled history fell to 0.233, and the random partner was flat. However, the model begins with a powerful expertise default: without history it chooses expertise 92.2% of the time. The useful learning happened on fairness partners (0.24 with history versus 0.05 without it) and risk partners (0.61 versus 0.10).

[FIG: fig_w1_v4_learning_by_condition.png] Figure 1. Qwen's match rate by round. The grey band marks held-out wording.

**Then I changed the partner.** After the silent switch, use of the new frame rose 0.108 and use of the old frame fell 0.105. That sounds promising, but the new frame never overtook the old one (difference 0.000, p = 0.50). The direction tells the real story: 34 of 40 switches into expertise adapted, nine into risk, and none into fairness. The model moved, but mostly back toward what it already preferred.

[FIG: fig_w2_v4_swap_by_transition.png] Figure 2. Qwen's change in new-frame and old-frame use after each type of switch.

**Now the difficult part.** I tried to break the result in three ways, with predictions written before each outcome. Rewording the prompt preserved the learning: gain 0.207 [0.110, 0.307], p = 0.0002 against no history. Gemma-4-31B did not replicate it: gain 0.040 [-0.007, 0.093]. Gemma repeated its frame almost regardless of feedback, 0.972 after success and 0.941 after failure, while Qwen changed more, 0.876 versus 0.693. Finally, when Qwen had to state a probability for every message, the learning disappeared: gain -0.020 [-0.053, 0.010]. In all 3,600 records its choice was an argmax of its own probabilities, but those probabilities stayed in the same order: expertise 0.69, risk 0.58, fairness 0.49.

[FIG: fig_w11_whole_story.png] Figure 3. The original result beside the prompt rewording, Gemma replication, and stated-belief arm.

**What I think this means.** There is evidence for a narrow, prompt- and model-dependent behavioural effect. There is not yet evidence that the model holds a separate, updateable model of its partner. A simpler rule still fits everything: repeat what worked, and otherwise fall back to expertise.

**What comes next.** The partner is simulated, the messages are pre-written, only one of two models learned, and the belief test changed two things at once. I would next test a third model, remove the expertise default with a two-frame design plus a distractor, and hide past predictions in the belief arm. Activation probes only make sense if the behavioural effect survives those tests.
